Appendix A

Definitions on Viscoelasticity

A.1 Introduction

The purpose of this appendix is to show the differences between classical fluids and non-Newtonian viscoelastic fluids. This appendix is based on several course notes [46] [47]. In general, non-Newtonian fluids are fluids whose characteristics change as a function of an external parameter or of mechanical stresses. Viscoelastic fluids belong to this last category.

If one considers an elastic solid and a viscous fluid, one quickly sees that their deformations are of an extremely different type. An elastic body returns to its natural undeformed state after the stresses applied to it cease. In contrast, viscous fluids have no tendency to return to any undeformed initial state. One can therefore naturally deduce that the elastic stress depends directly on the deformation, while the viscous stress depends only on the deformation rate, that is, on its time derivative. A fluid that behaves like a mixture of these two characters is called a viscoelastic fluid. In general, a fluid can be considered non-Newtonian if its constitutive equation, relating the stress tensor to the strain-rate tensor, is no longer directly proportional to the latter.

A.2 Conservation laws and constitutive laws

In continuum mechanics, for a fluid whose characteristics do not vary with temperature, one uses the Cauchy form of the momentum conservation equation: \[\begin{equation} \rho \left( \partial_t\,{\mathbf{u}}+{\mathbf{u}}\cdot\nabla{\mathbf{u}} \right)= \nabla \cdot{\mathbf{\sigma}}+ F \end{equation}\] where \(F\) is an external forcing term, and the mass conservation equation: \[\begin{equation} \partial_t \,\rho + \nabla\cdot {\mathbf{u}}=0. \end{equation}\] The unknowns in these four equations number ten if one considers that the tensor \(\sigma\) is symmetric. To close the system, we therefore need six additional equations, often deduced from experiment: the constitutive laws.

A.3 Conditions imposed on constitutive laws

The constitutive equations, or constitutive laws, give relations between the stress \(\sigma\), the fluid velocity \({\mathbf{u}}(r,t)\) through the strain-rate tensor \({\dot D}\), its temperature, etc. For each fluid element, these relations will involve only local quantities and possibly spatial and temporal derivatives. Furthermore, the entire history of the material element considered, up to time \(t\), may come into play. For viscoelastic fluids, this relation is often nonlinear and depends on the complete history of the fluid. The constitutive equations must satisfy imposed conditions which can be classified into two large families: thermodynamic conditions and conditions of invariance under change of axis.

The thermodynamic conditions result from the first and second laws of thermodynamics. They can be summarized as: “The total dissipated power must be positive (zero in the reversible case)”. These conditions will not be developed here; we will only be interested in “isothermal” fluids.

The conditions of invariance under change of axis come from the fact that the equations must be written in a form independent of the orientation of the axes (tensorial invariance) and of the motion of the frame (objectivity); they must also be compatible with the symmetries of the material (isotropy).

A.3.1 Tensorial invariance

The constitutive equations must only contain quantities that can be put in a form independent of the orientation of the chosen axes, that is, having identical meaning in two systems of axes fixed relative to each other, which can be considered a good definition of what a tensor is. There can be two types of tensors in a constitutive equation: the material tensors related to the material (conductivity tensor, permeability tensor, …) and the physical tensors related to physical quantities. If the constitutive equation is written: \[\begin{equation} \label{tt1} F\left(M_{ijk},T_{ij},V_{i},\dots\right)=0, \end{equation}\] with \(M\), \(T\) and \(V\) tensors, then under an orthonormal change of coordinates, \[\begin{equation} \label{chcoo} x'_i=P_{i\,j}\,x_j+ C_i, \qquad \left(P_{i\,k}\,P_{j\,k}=\delta_{i\,j}\right) \end{equation}\] equation(A.3) must remain satisfied when \(M_{ijk},T_{ij},V{i}\) are replaced by \[\begin{equation} M'_{ijk}=P_{i\alpha}\,P_{j\beta}\,P_{k\gamma}\,M_{\alpha\beta\gamma},\quad T'_{ij}=P_{i\alpha}\,P_{j\beta}\,T_{\alpha\beta},\quad V'_i=P_{i\alpha}\,V_{\alpha}. \end{equation}\]

A.3.2 Symmetry of matter, Isotropy

Under any change of axis respecting the symmetries of the material, the material tensors remain invariant. The constitutive equations must therefore remain unchanged if only a change of basis is applied to the physical tensors.

Maximum symmetry is obtained when the material is isotropic. The material tensors then remain unchanged under any transformation of the orthonormal group \(O(D)\). Such tensors are said to be isotropic. It is then easy to show that the only isotropic second-order tensors are the spherical tensors (proportional to \(\delta_{ij}\)), and that fourth-order tensors must necessarily have the form: \[\begin{equation} T_{\alpha\beta\gamma\delta}=\lambda\,\left(\delta_{\alpha\beta}\,\delta_{\gamma\delta}+ \delta_{\alpha\gamma}\,\delta_{\beta\delta}+\delta_{\alpha\delta}\,\delta_{\beta\gamma} \right)+\mu \delta_{\alpha\beta}\delta_{\gamma\delta}\delta_{\beta\gamma}. \end{equation}\] When the material is isotropic, the constitutive equations must remain unchanged under any transformation of the orthogonal group. We thus have, with \(T\) and \(V\) material tensors: \[F\left(T_{ij},V_{i},\dots\right)=0 \Rightarrow F\left(P_{i\alpha}\,P_{j\beta}\,T_{\alpha\beta}, P_{i\alpha}\,V_{\alpha}\right)=0\] for any orthogonal matrix \(P\). This implies that one can omit the material tensors in the constitutive equations in an isotropic medium. In general, the media studied in continuum mechanics are considered as isotropic; the constitutive equations thus contain only physical tensors and are independent of the material element considered.

A.3.3 Objectivity

The constitutive equations must be independent of the motion of the observer; they must therefore be invariant under the transformation defined by equation(A.4) when \(P_{i\,j}\) and \(C_i\) now depend on time. This is the objectivity principle. This principle leads to introducing in the constitutive equations only objective quantities. One can show that the position of a material point is not objective: \[\begin{equation} x'_i=P_{i\,j}(t)\,x_j+ C_i(t), \end{equation}\] that the velocity of this point is not either: \[\begin{equation} u'_i=P_{i\,j}(t)\,u_j+\dot{P_{i\,j}}(t)\,x_j+ \dot{C_i}(t). \end{equation}\] On the other hand, one can verify that the strain-rate tensor: \[\begin{equation} \dot{D}_{ij}={\partial u_i \over \partial x_j} + {\partial u_j \over \partial x_i} \end{equation}\] is objective, but the rotation-rate tensor of a volume element \(\Omega\): \[\begin{equation} \Omega_{ij}={\partial u_i \over \partial x_j} - {\partial u_j \over \partial x_i} \end{equation}\] is not. Let \({\mathcal{W}}\) be an objective quantity of order 2; it can be shown that an objective time derivative is necessarily of the form: \[\begin{equation} {\partial_{obj.} {\mathcal{W}}\over \partial t}={D\,{\mathcal{W}}\over D t}+a\,{\mathcal{W}}\cdot {\mathcal{K}}+(d-1)\,{\mathcal{K}}\cdot{\mathcal{W}}+(a-1)\, {\mathcal{W}}\cdot{\mathcal{K}}^T+d\,{\mathcal{K}}^T\cdot{\mathcal{W}}, \end{equation}\] with \(\displaystyle ({\mathcal{K}})_{ij}={\partial u_i\over \partial x_j}\), and \(D\) indicating the material derivative. One can then choose the parameters to obtain different types of derivatives; some choices can be noted:

  • if \(a=d=0\) then this derivative is the convective derivative: \[\begin{equation} {\partial_{obj.} {\mathcal{W}}\over \partial t}={\mathcal{W}}_{[1]}={D\,{\mathcal{W}}\over D t}-\left( {\mathcal{W}}\cdot{\mathcal{K}}^T+ {\mathcal{K}}\cdot{\mathcal{W}}\right), \end{equation}\]

  • if \(a=d=1/2\) then this derivative is the so-called Jaumann derivative: \[\begin{equation} {\partial_{obj.} {\mathcal{W}}\over \partial t}={\mathcal{W}}_{[1]}^J={D\,{\mathcal{W}}\over D t}+{1\over 2}\left({\mathcal{W}}\cdot\Omega+\Omega^T \cdot{\mathcal{W}}\right), \end{equation}\] which can also be written: \[\begin{equation} {\mathcal{W}}_{[1]}^J={\mathcal{W}}_{[1]}+{1\over 2}\left({\mathcal{W}}\cdot{\dot D}+ {\dot D}^T\cdot{\mathcal{W}}\right). \end{equation}\]

The derivatives we will denote \({\mathcal{W}}_{[i]}\) will be convective derivatives of order \(i\); by continuity and for consistency reasons, we will take: \[\begin{equation} {\mathcal{W}}_{[0]}={\mathcal{W}}. \end{equation}\]

A.4 Admissible constitutive laws

The previously indicated conditions, although numerous, do not allow writing a global constitutive equation valid for all fluids. One can nevertheless classify them into several groups. The simplest group is the quasi-linear group.

A.4.1 Quasi-linear laws - Oldroyd-B law

A generic quasi-linear constitutive law containing time derivatives can be written from the previous indications in the form: \[\begin{equation} \label{generique} \lambda_i\,\sigma_{[i]}=-\mu_0\,\left(\lambda'_j\,D_{[j]} \right) \qquad i\geq 0,\, j\geq 1. \end{equation}\] with \(\mu_0\) a viscosity, \(\lambda_i\) and \(\lambda'_i\) functions of the elementary invariants of the stress tensor \(\sigma\) and the strain tensor \(D\). It is interesting to note that: \[\begin{equation} D_{[n+1]}={\partial^n {\dot D}\over \partial t^n}. \end{equation}\] Indeed, one can then pass from a viscoelastic fluid to a viscous fluid and recover the Navier-Stokes equations by taking \(i=0\) and \(j=1\), or, more generally, \(j=i+1\) and \(\lambda_j=\lambda_{i+1}\)..

One can then give a definition of a quasi-linear viscoelastic fluid: A viscoelastic fluid is a non-Newtonian fluid whose constitutive law is of the form defined by equation (A.16).

Starting from this law, it is possible, with the valuable aid of experiment, to determine a constitutive equation corresponding to the fluid considered. Among these, some have become more famous than others.

The so-called Oldroyd-B model [1], or convective Jeffreys model, is given by: \[\begin{equation} \label{oldroyd-B} \sigma+\tau_1\,\sigma_{[1]}=-\mu_0\,\left(D_{[1]} + \tau_2\,D_{[2]} \right). \end{equation}\] The constant \(\tau_1\) is called the relaxation time and \(\tau_2\) the retardation time. One can note that, if they are both zero and, in the limit, equal, one recovers the constitutive equation of a classical Newtonian fluid. This equation is commonly used in numerical modeling. This model also serves as a basis for constructing nonlinear models. The simplest linear viscoelastic model is the Maxwell model constructed by taking \(\tau_2=0\).

A.4.2 Introduction of invariants

One can complicate the models already obtained by introducing invariants of the system into the constitutive equations.

A.4.2.1 Elementary invariants

It can be shown that the characteristic polynomial of a matrix \(({\mathcal{W}})_{ij}\), associated with the tensor \({\mathcal{W}}\), is invariant under change of basis. Let \({\mathcal{Q}}\) be the change-of-basis matrix satisfying \({\mathcal{Q}}\cdot{\mathcal{Q}}^{-1}={\mathcal{I}}\). Then the characteristic polynomial of the matrix \({\mathcal{W}}'={\mathcal{Q}}^{-1}\cdot{\mathcal{W}}\cdot{\mathcal{Q}}\) satisfies: \[\begin{eqnarray} P_c(\lambda)=\det\left({\mathcal{W}}'-\lambda\,{\mathcal{I}}\right)&=&\det\left({\mathcal{Q}}^{-1}\cdot{\mathcal{W}}\cdot{\mathcal{Q}}- \lambda\,{\mathcal{I}}\right) \nonumber\\ &=&\det\left({\mathcal{Q}}^{-1}\cdot\left( {\mathcal{W}}-\lambda \,{\mathcal{I}}\right)\cdot{\mathcal{Q}}\right)\\ &=&\det\left({\mathcal{Q}}^{-1} \right)\,\det\left({\mathcal{W}}-\lambda \,{\mathcal{I}}\right)\, \det\left({\mathcal{Q}} \right) \nonumber\\ &=&\det\left( {\mathcal{W}}-\lambda \,{\mathcal{I}}\right) \nonumber \end{eqnarray}\]

Moreover, for a second-order tensor in three dimensions, one can write the characteristic polynomial \[\begin{equation} P_c(\lambda)=-\lambda^3+M_I\,\lambda^2-M_{II}\,\lambda+M_{III}, \end{equation}\] and \(M_I,\,M_{II}\mbox{ and}\,M_{III}\) are called the elementary invariants associated with the tensor \({\mathcal{W}}\), and we have: \[\begin{eqnarray} M_I&=&{\mathrm{Tr}}\left({\mathcal{W}}\right)\nonumber\\ M_{II}&=&{1\over 2}\left[{\mathrm{Tr}}^2\left({\mathcal{W}}\right)-{\mathrm{Tr}}\left({\mathcal{W}}^2\right) \right]\\ M_{III}&=&\det\left({\mathcal{W}}\right)={1\over 3}\left[{\mathrm{Tr}}\left({\mathcal{W}}^3\right)-M_I\,{\mathrm{Tr}}\left({\mathcal{W}}^2\right)+ M_{II}\,{\mathrm{Tr}}\left({\mathcal{W}}\right) \right]\nonumber \end{eqnarray}\]

A.4.2.2 White-Metzner model

The White-Metzner model [2] is a variant of the Oldroyd-B model in which the viscosity depends on the invariants of \(\dot D\): \[\begin{equation} \label{White-Metzner} \sigma+{\mu({\dot\gamma})\over G}\,\sigma_{[1]}=-\mu({\dot\gamma} )\,D_{[1]} \end{equation}\] where \({\dot\gamma}\) is related to the invariants of \(\dot D\) by \[\begin{equation} \label{shear-rate} {\dot\gamma}=\sqrt{{1\over 2} {\mathrm{Tr}}\left({\dot D}^2\right)}=\sqrt{{{\dot D}_I^2 \over 2}- {\dot D}_{II}}, \end{equation}\] \(G\) a constant and the viscosity \(\mu({\dot\gamma})\) a function often chosen as a power law with two parameters \(m\) expressed in \(\mathrm{Pa.s}^n\), and \(n\) dimensionless: \[\begin{equation} \label{lpuissance} \mu({\dot\gamma})=m\,{\dot \gamma}^{n-1}. \end{equation}\] When \(n>1\), the fluid is said to be dilatant (shear thickening); when \(n<1\) the fluid is said to be pseudo-plastic (shear thinning). The vast majority of fluids are of pseudo-plastic type, that is, their viscosity decreases with stress. The motion of the fluid breaks its internal structure.

Another law can be chosen, such as the Carreau-Yasuda law [4] [5], with five free parameters \((\mu_0,\mu_\infty,\lambda,n \mbox{ and }a)\): \[\begin{equation} \label{carreau-yasuda} {\mu-\mu_\infty \over \mu_0-\mu_\infty}=\left[ 1+\left( \lambda{\dot\gamma}^a \right) \right]^{(n-1)/a}. \end{equation}\] The power law poorly represents the viscosity of the fluid for very low stress; the Carreau-Yasuda law fills this gap.

A.4.3 Nonlinear laws

One can complicate the constitutive laws at will, but a remark by Oldroyd about the Oldroyd-B model will lead to a form of law possessing eight free parameters, and allowing the modeling of a considerable number of viscoelastic fluids. The Oldroyd-B model intrinsically contains quadratic terms in velocity gradient, but not in a systematic way; one can therefore include those that are missing manually, which gives the so-called “Oldroyd-8-constants” equation: \[\begin{eqnarray} \label{oldroyd-8-constants} \sigma+\tau_1\,\sigma_{[1]}+{1\over 2}\, \tau_3 \,\left(D_{[1]} \cdot\sigma+\sigma\cdot D_{[1]} \right)+ {1\over 5}\, \tau_5\, \sigma_I\,D_{[1]}+{1\over 2}\, \tau_6\,\left( \sigma:D_{[1]}\right){\mathcal{I}}\nonumber \\ \qquad = -\mu_0\, \left[D_{[1]} + \tau_2\,D_{[2]}\right] -\mu_0\, \left[\tau_4\,\left( D_{[1]}\cdot D_{[1]} \right) +{1\over 2}\,\tau_7\,\left( D_{[1]}:D_{[1]} \right){\mathcal{I}}\right]. \end{eqnarray}\] The first two terms of the expression on the left of the equal sign and the first two terms on the right constitute the Oldroyd-B equation. There are validity conditions for the various constants of equation (A.26). We will not develop them here.

A.5 Linear laws and complex viscosity

A.5.1 Laws in integral form

The previous laws can be put, in a simple way or not, in integral form. In general, one shows that one can rewrite a linear constitutive equation in the form: \[\begin{equation} \label{comportementintegralM} \sigma(t)=\int_{-\infty}^t{M(t-t')\,D_{[0]}(t,t')\,dt'} \end{equation}\] where \(M(t,t')\) is the memory function of the fluid. One also defines the relaxation modulus function \(G(t,t')\) by: \[\begin{equation} \label{comportementintegralG} \sigma(t)=-\int_{-\infty}^t{G(t-t')\,{\dot D}_{[0]}(t')\,dt'}, \end{equation}\] one then has, by integration by parts, the relation: \[\begin{equation} M(t-t')={\partial G(t-t') \over \partial t}. \end{equation}\]

A.5.2 Jeffreys laws

To facilitate the integration of the Oldroyd-B model, we will make the following simplification; let us replace the convective derivatives by partial derivatives, this amounts to neglecting the quadratic terms in velocity gradient. One then obtains the Jeffreys constitutive equation: \[\begin{equation} \label{jeffreys} \sigma+\,\tau_1 {\partial \sigma \over \partial t}=\mu_0\,\left({\dot D}+\tau_2\,{\partial {\dot D} \over \partial t} \right). \end{equation}\]

One can then integrate equation (A.30), and its relaxation modulus is then given by: \[\begin{equation} \label{modrelaxjeffreys} G(t-t')=\mu_0\left(1-{\tau_2 \over \tau_1} \right)\,e^{-(t-t')/\tau_1} + 2 {\mu_0\,\tau_2 \over \tau_1}\,\delta(t-t'), \end{equation}\] where the function \(\delta(t-t')\) is the Dirac function.

The Jeffreys equation is very often used in rheology. It allows modeling the internal layers of the Earth’s crust for example [3]. It is mainly this equation that we will try to model by the lattice Boltzmann method.

A.5.3 Complex viscosity

Problems involving time-periodic effects can be simplified by switching to complex notation. Indeed, in Fourier space, a Jeffreys-type equation (A.30) can be written \[\begin{equation} \label{jeffreys-complexe} \sigma\,\left(1+i\,\omega\,\tau_1\right)=\mu_0\,{\dot D}\left(1+i\,\omega\,\tau_2 \right), \end{equation}\] or more simply, by analogy with Newtonian fluids and with a complex viscosity: \[\begin{equation} \label{jeffreys-complexe2} \sigma=\mu^*\,{\dot D}\qquad\mbox{with }\mu^*=\mu_0\,{1+i\,\omega\,\tau_1\over 1+i\,\omega\,\tau_2 }. \end{equation}\]

The complex viscosity can also be written in a simpler form: \[\begin{equation} \label{visco-complexe} \mu^*=\mu_\infty+{\mu_0-\mu_\infty \over 1+i\,\omega\,\tau_1} \end{equation}\] with \[\begin{equation} \mu_\infty=\mu_0\,{\tau_2 \over \tau_1} \end{equation}\] This formulation allows problems to be treated with methods similar to those used for Newtonian fluids, and it will therefore be very commonly used in the rest of our study.
