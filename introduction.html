General Introduction

Fluid mechanics groups together the disciplines whose purpose is to describe and predict the motion of gases and liquids. This description proceeds through the derivation of partial differential equations that govern the motion of fluids.

In a general way, mechanicians consider the fluid as a macroscopically continuous medium. Indeed, each macroscopic fluid element contains a considerable number of particles. To each physical point of the fluid, identified by its coordinates in space, one then attaches quantities that characterize its state: the velocity \(u=u(x,y,z,t)\), and two thermodynamic quantities linked by an equation of state of the fluid considered, such as the density \(\rho(x,y,z,t)\) and the pressure \(P(x,y,z,t)\) or else the temperature \(T(x,y,z,t)\). Thus, these partial differential equations derive from three main conservation laws. Mass conservation is expressed through the continuity equation, which reflects the fact that the variation of the amount of matter of a fluid element is equal to the mass fluxes that cross its walls. No approximation on the nature of the fluid is necessary to obtain this first equation. The second equation accounts for the conservation of momentum. If one considers that there is no internal dissipation of energy in the fluid due to viscosity, nor any heat exchange between the various parts, one obtains the so-called Euler equation. It is the fundamental equation of perfect fluids. If, now, one takes viscous dissipation effects into account, it is necessary to make an assumption on the form of the viscous stress tensor. This assumption, which is called the constitutive law, depends on the nature of the fluid considered. The simplest of these constitutive laws corresponds to the so-called Newtonian assumption; in this case the momentum conservation equation is called the Navier-Stokes equation. Depending on the nature of the fluid, one will choose an appropriate constitutive law, but it will always remain an approximation of the behavior of the real fluid under specific conditions of use.

The third equation expresses the conservation of energy. In the same way as for the continuity equation, it stems from the fact that the variation of energy in a given volume is equal to the energy fluxes through the walls of the element of volume considered. It depends strongly on the previous assumption on the nature of the fluid.

Some fluid materials used in industry do not meet the criteria of so-called Newtonian fluids. Among these fluids, some are called viscoelastic fluids. A viscoelastic fluid is a fluid which possesses both the viscous character of fluids and the elastic character of solids. To describe it, we will have to specify the constitutive law that characterizes it. Thus we will address the problem of the form of the constitutive laws that are admissible in general for physics, and for a viscoelastic fluid in particular.

The mechanician must then set up techniques that allow him to obtain good numerical approximations of the solutions of the previous partial differential equations. The main resolution methods at his disposal are the finite difference, finite element, and spectral methods.

The numerical method used as the basis of this thesis is of a quite different nature. Our approach is the simulation at the microscopic level of the evolution of the fluid particles constituting the medium. That is, we directly model the mesoscopic behavior of the fluid without going through the stages of determination and resolution of macroscopic equations. In our models, the fluid particles move and collide, imitating as closely as possible the real world. The problem is arduous because the resolution of the equations of motion of the fluid particles is prohibitive for a domain of significant size. The simplifying idea is then to spa­tia­lly discretize the simulated domain. By reducing the number of degrees of freedom of the problem, one correspondingly enlarges the domain that can be simulated numerically. This discretization, while it simplifies the construction of the model, nevertheless complicates the theoretical link with the real medium. It is by applying this idea not only to space but also to time that Hardy, de Pazzis and Pomeau created the first lattice gas and developed the foundations of the corresponding theory in 1973. This model does not faithfully reproduce hydrodynamic phenomena and one had to wait until 1985 for Frisch, Hasslacher and Pomeau to develop a model allowing the simulation of a fluid medium satisfying a posteriori the macroscopic Navier-Stokes equations.

Lattice gas models then proved the validity of the “inverse” approach to resolving the Navier-Stokes equations, consisting in simulating a set of simplified particles interacting while satisfying the physical conservation principles. This approach, summarized as “Microscopic simplicity can generate macroscopic complexity”, is one of the essential contributions of lattice gases to fluid mechanics.

We will first look at a history of lattice gases and of the progression towards the method that we will use throughout this thesis, the lattice Boltzmann equation. Indeed, this young method has been in constant evolution for the past ten years or so and seems today to have reached a state of maturity allowing it to compete with so-called more classical methods. We will describe the transition from the first lattice Boltzmann models considered by Mc Namara and Zanetti [15] towards more recent models. This evolution will of course lead us to understand how the current models are built, including the well-known B.G.K. model, which turns out to be a particular case of the general models. The formulation we have just set up then allows us to approach more simply the study of the boundary conditions induced by the presence of solid walls. Thus, we will make the position of these walls independent of the fluid characteristics, which was one of the major flaws of this type of boundary condition.

The constitutive laws used during numerical simulation by the lattice Boltzmann method are by default of Newtonian type. This stems from the fact that the viscous modes are relaxed towards an equilibrium state with a very short relaxation time. Thus the fluid almost instantaneously loses its “viscous memory”.

We will therefore add additional characteristics to the fluid that allow us to modify the constitutive laws of the simulated fluids. The point is actually to give the fluid a viscous memory by the addition of new modes coupled to the viscous modes with a long relaxation time.

Thus, we will extend the lattice Boltzmann method to allow the simulation of isotropic Jeffreys-type viscoelastic fluids. We will perform several numerical experiments which will allow us to better characterize the simulated fluid and thus demonstrate the validity of our assumptions.

In a further step, we will try to understand why the addition of two modes coupled to the viscous modes and possessing very long relaxation times leads us to a Jeffreys-type fluid. Finally, we will explore some applications of our models allowing the simulation of even more complex fluids possessing nonlinear characteristics.
