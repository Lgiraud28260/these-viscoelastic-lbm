Appendix C

Introduction of a Passive Scalar - Modeling of Miscible Fluids

C.1 Presentation of the model

The modeling of miscible fluids has already been introduced in the past [33] by considering two distinct fluids \(A\) and \(B\). The trick then used was to consider on the one hand the fluid made of \(A+B\) and on the other hand the one made of \(A-B\). While the fluid \(A+B\) is treated as a classical fluid with conservation of mass and momentum, the fluid \(A-B\) is considered as a fluid possessing only conservation of mass, its momentum being relaxed with a time constant \(\tau_D\). The diffusion of one fluid into the other is then characterized by the diffusion coefficient \(\displaystyle D=c_s^2\left( \tau_D-\frac{1}{2}\right)\). The major drawback of this method is the memory space required for its operation. Indeed, it requires twice as much memory to model the mixing of two fluids, and the problem becomes even more tricky when a third fluid is introduced.

A more efficient method has been introduced for lattice gases [43], and involves only one scalar per grid point. This scalar represents the concentration of the fluid at point \(r\) of the considered space and at time \(t\), denoted \(C(r,t)\). The evolution of this concentration is described by the following equation: \[\begin{equation} \label{diff-gsr} C(r,t+1)=\alpha {C(r,t)\over \rho(r,t)}+\sum_{i=1}^b{(n_i(r-c_i,t)-\alpha/b)\, C(r-c_i,t) \over \rho(r-c_i,t)}. \end{equation}\] The number \(\alpha\) can be interpreted as the probability that the fluid concentration remains identical at the next time step at the same space point \(r\). The second term on the right-hand side of equation (C.1) is interpreted as the probability that the concentration propagates in the other directions \(c_i\) of the lattice at time \(t+1\). The quantity \(n(r,t)\) quantifies the presence or absence of a particle at point \(r\) at time \(t\), and \(b\) the number of discrete directions of the lattice. This equation can be transcribed for the case of lattice Boltzmann gases as: \[\begin{equation} \label{DIFFB} C(r,t+1)=\alpha C(r,t)+\sum_{i=0}^b{\left({{\tilde N}_i^{(0)}(r,t)\over \rho} -t_p\, \alpha\right) }C(r-c_i,t), \end{equation}\] where \({\tilde N}_i^{(0)}(r,t)\) denotes the linear part of the equilibrium quantity \(N_i^{(0)}(r,t)\) whose expression was given in chapter 3 of the first part for the two-dimensional nine-velocity model and in chapter 4 for the three-dimensional nineteen-velocity model. We will therefore have: \[\begin{equation} {\tilde N}_i^{(0)}(r,t)=t_p\,\rho\,\left(1+{j_\alpha\,c_{i\alpha} \over c_s^2}\right), \end{equation}\] the coefficients \(t_p\) then depend on the model, and for the nine-velocity model we have: \[\begin{equation} t_0={3 - 5 c_s^2\over 3}\qquad t_1={c_s^2\over 3} \quad\mbox{and}\quad t_2={c_s^ 2\over 12}, \end{equation}\] and for the nineteen-velocity model, we have: \[\begin{equation} t_0=1- 2 c_s^2 \qquad t_1={c_s^2\over 6} \quad\mbox{and}\quad t_2={c_s^2\over 12 }. \end{equation}\] The remarkable relations for the coefficients \(t_p\) on these two lattices are: \[\begin{equation} \sum_{i=0}^b{t_p}=1,\quad\sum_{i=0}^b{t_p\, c_{i\alpha}}=0,\quad\sum_{i=0}^b{t_p\, c_{i\alpha}c_{i \beta}}=c_s^2\,\delta_{\alpha\beta}\mbox{ and } \end{equation}\] \[\begin{equation} \sum_{i=0}^b{t_p\, c_{i\alpha}c_{i\beta}c_{i\gamma}}=0,\quad\sum_{i=0}^b{t_p\, c_{i\alpha}c_ {i\beta}c_{i\gamma}c_{i\delta} }={c_s^2\over 3}\left(\delta_{\alpha\beta} \delta_{\gamma\delta}+ \delta_{\alpha\gamma} \delta_{\beta\delta} +\delta_{\alpha\delta} \delta_{\beta\gamma} \right). \end{equation}\]

Taking these relations into account and performing a second-order Taylor expansion in space and first-order in time of equation (C.2), one easily obtains the macroscopic evolution equation of the concentration, called the advection-diffusion equation: \[\begin{equation} \label{ADVEC-DIFF} {\partial C \over \partial t}+{\mathbf{u}}\cdot\nabla C=D \,\Delta C, \end{equation}\] where \(u=\sum_{i=0}^b{N_i\,c_i}/\rho\) is the local velocity of the carrier fluid and \(D\) the diffusion coefficient given by: \[\begin{equation} D=c_s^2\,\left({1-\alpha\over 2}\right). \end{equation}\]

C.2 Simulations

We define the Peclet number, \(P_e\), as the ratio between the convective time and the diffusive time of our system: \[\begin{equation} P_e=\frac{u\,L}{D}, \end{equation}\] where \(u\) and \(L\) are respectively a characteristic velocity and length of the system.

First, let us consider a very small Peclet number, and in the limit zero. The numerical simulation we will perform is identical to that described in Flekkøy’s thesis [33]. It concerns the study of the diffusion of a concentration wave over time, given by: \[\begin{equation} C(r,t)=\frac{C_{max.}}{2}\,\left(1+e^{-D\,k^2\,t}\sin \left(k(x-ut)\right) \right). \end{equation}\] We can then measure \(D\), and in the case of zero Peclet, \(u=0\), we obtain the graph of figure C.1 where the abscissa is the value of the theoretical diffusion coefficient and the ordinate its measured value as well as the line \(y=x\). We then obtain an excellent agreement between the predicted value and the simulation over a very wide range of diffusion values. The maximum relative error observed for this simulation is less than \(1.0\times10^{-5}\) for \(D=1.0\times10^{-8}\).

When the Peclet number is not zero, the previous curve becomes less good and depends on the value of the advection velocity, \(u\), parallel to the wave vector, through its square. One can then realize that our model, in the same way as that of Flekkøy, is not Galilean invariant at every order of precision. The actual diffusion coefficient can then be estimated as: \[\begin{equation} \label{corec} D=c_s^2\,\left({1-\alpha\over 2}\right)\,-\frac{u^2}{2.0}. \end{equation}\] This formula remains valid over a wide range of diffusion values; thus in figure C.2, we have plotted on this graph only the diffusions giving a relative error less than \(0.01\) compared to the value given by (C.12), for an advection velocity \(u=0.05\).

One can note that this kind of correction is of a higher order than the order used to perform a Chapman-Enskog expansion. Equation (C.8) thus remains valid at the order at which the remainder of our fluid’s behavior is considered. This model is therefore Galilean invariant at this order of precision.

In conclusion, this method gives results of the same order of precision as Flekkøy’s method, while allowing a very appreciable memory saving, especially in three dimensions. The passive scalar \(C(r,t)\) can also represent a temperature. One can then, for example, vary the speed of sound, or the viscosity, in the fluid as a function of \(C\), and thus model fluids in which the temperature plays an active role. By this method, one can also study mixtures of viscoelastic fluids with characteristics differentiated by the values of \(C\).
