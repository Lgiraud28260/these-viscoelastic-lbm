Part I · Foundations of the Lattice Boltzmann Method — Chapter 2

Lattice Boltzmann gases

After this introduction, we will address more specifically the model we have used, called the lattice Boltzmann model, which is in fact an extension of the previous Boolean models.

2.1 General characteristics

One of the problems of Boolean models (and paradoxically one of their strengths) is the significant background noise imposed by the use of discontinuous quantities. The basic idea of the Boltzmann model is to take into account directly the averaged quantities. The basic element will no longer be the discrete value \(n_i\) but its spatial average \(N_i\) performed over a given number of nodes. For the Boltzmann model directly translated from the Boolean model, this quantity is continuous, between 0 and 1, and constitutes in fact a distribution, or probability of presence of particles in direction \(i\).

2.1.1 Boltzmann hypothesis

The problem is to determine how this set of dis­tri­bu­tions evolves. Boltzmann’s molecular chaos hypothesis, coming from statistical mechanics, allows us to assume that two distributions associated with two different directions are uncorrelated before interaction (a collision in the case of interest). Outside the collision step, everything thus proceeds as if the distributions were independent.

The propagation step, and all boundary condition steps, are identical to those of a Boolean lattice gas; only the nature of the transported quantities varies. The collision step is, on the other hand, different.

The main characteristics of a lattice Boltzmann model can be summarized by the following propositions:

The simulation space is discretized by a regular “crystalline” lattice. The distributions can only be located at the nodes of this lattice.

The distributions associated with two different directions are not correlated before interaction.

The distributions possess the same “velocity” and “jump” at each time step from one site to one of the nearest neighbor sites.

The dis­tri­bu­tions located at a single node can interact while conserving mass (the probability density) and momentum.

2.2 Evolution equation

The evolution equation of a lattice Boltzmann gas is quite simple to formulate. It is in fact a spatial average of the microdynamic equation of a Boolean lattice gas, performed with the Boltzmann hypothesis, i.e.

\[\begin{equation} \label{mesoscopique} N_i(\vec{x}+\vec{c}_i, t + 1) = N_i(\vec{x}, t) + \Delta_i \left[ {\mathbf{N}}(\vec{x}, t) \right]. \end{equation}\]

The problem is however to express the collision operator \(\Delta\).

2.2.1 Complete collision operator

The most direct way is to take the formulation of section 1.7. Indeed, one can note that the collision operator resulting from the averaging of the Boolean operator can be written in the same way as it by the Boltzmann hypothesis  [22]. It is then a matter of replacing in the expressions \(n_i\) by \(N_i\). The result for each configuration is a real number between 0 and 1 (contrary to the discrete values -1 and 1 of the collision operator of Boolean models).

One thus obtains, in the same way, an operator acting on dis­tri­bu­tions, corresponding to particular microscopic collision configurations.

This method is simple to apply when the number of directions is small. However, if one uses a more complex lattice (FCHC for example) the number of possible combinations (\(2^{24}\)) makes the direct expression of the collision operator difficult.

2.2.2 Linearized equation

A simple solution consists in making a hypothesis of spatial and temporal quasi-equilibrium  [19]. Placing oneself at low Mach number and low Knudsen number, one can consider small-amplitude perturbations. This allows one to decompose the distribution vector into an equilibrium term and an out-of-equilibrium term, that is

\[\begin{equation} {\mathbf{N}} = {\mathbf{N}}^{eq} + {\mathbf{N}}^{neq} \end{equation}\]

In the low-velocity hypothesis, one can perform a Taylor expansion of \({\mathbf{N}}\) at equilibrium as a function of a small parameter which will here be the velocity \(u\); one then has:

\[\begin{equation} {\mathbf{N}}^{eq} = {\mathbf{N}}_0^{eq}+{\mathbf{N}}_1^{eq}+{\mathbf{N}}_2^{eq}+\dots+{\mathbf{N}}_k^{eq}+{\mathcal{O}}(u^{k+1}) \end{equation}\]

Expanding the collision operator in the same way, one obtains, at order 2 in velocity,

\[\begin{equation} \label{celleaudessus} \Delta({\mathbf{N}}) = \Delta({\mathbf{N}}_0^{eq}) + \left( \frac{\partial \Delta}{\partial {\mathbf{N}}} \right )_{{\mathbf{N}}_0^{eq}} \left( {\mathbf{N}}_1^{eq} + {\mathbf{N}}_2^{eq} + {\mathbf{N}}^{neq} \right) + \frac{1}{2}{\mathbf{N}}_1^{eq}\left( \frac{\partial^2\Delta}{\partial{\mathbf{N}}^2}\right)_{{\mathbf{N}}_0^{eq}}{\mathbf{N}}_1^{eq} + \dots \end{equation}\]

By definition, at equilibrium the system is stationary; one therefore has

\[\begin{equation} \Delta({\mathbf{N}}^{eq}) = 0 = \Delta^{(0)} + \Delta^{(1)}\left({\mathbf{N}}_1^{eq} + ({\mathbf{N}}_2^{eq} + 0) \right)+ \frac{1}{2}{\mathbf{N}}_1^{eq}\Delta^{(2)}{\mathbf{N}}_1^{eq} + \dots \end{equation}\] with by definition \[\Delta^{(i)}=(\partial^i\Delta)/(\partial{\mathbf{N}}^i),\] substituting in (2.4), one obtains \(\Delta({\mathbf{N}}) = \Delta^{(1)}{\mathbf{N}}^{neq}\).

Let us take by definition, with the sign conventions allowing one to choose positive eigenvalues of the matrix \(\Omega\):

\[\begin{equation} \Omega_{ij} = -\left(\frac{\partial \Delta_i}{\partial N_j}\right)_{N_i^{eq}} \end{equation}\]

The evolution equation (2.1) then becomes the linearized evolution equation

\[\begin{equation} \label{linearisee} N_i(\vec{x} + \vec{c}_i, t + 1) = N_i(\vec{x}, t) - \sum_{j = 0}^{b} \Omega_{ij}N_j^{neq}(\vec{x}, t) \end{equation}\]

with \(b\) the number of directions of the lattice used, \(N_j^{neq} = N_j - N_j^{eq}\).

The matrix \(\Omega\) is called the collision matrix.

2.2.2.1 Hypotheses

The hypotheses made to arrive at this result are:

  1. Non-correlation of distributions before interaction (Boltzmann).

  2. Local quasi-equilibrium.

    • Low Mach and Knudsen numbers.

    • Small density and velocity gradients.

  3. Continuity of the functions and their derivatives.

2.2.3 Properties of the collision matrix

In the same way as the collision operator \(\Delta\), the collision matrix \(\Omega\) must ensure the verification of the conservation principles.

  • Conservation of mass

    The probability density must be conserved. That is \[\begin{equation} \sum_i\sum_j\Omega_{ij}N_j^{neq} = 0, \quad \forall {\mathbf{N}}^{neq} \end{equation}\] That is \[\begin{equation} \sum_i\Omega_{ij} = 0, \quad \forall j \end{equation}\]

  • Conservation of momentum

    Similarly \[\begin{equation} \sum_i\vec{c}_i\sum_j\Omega_{ij}N_j^{neq} = \vec{0}, \quad \forall {\mathbf{N}}^{neq} \end{equation}\] That is \[\begin{equation} \sum_i\Omega_{ij}\vec{c}_i = \vec{0}, \quad \forall j \end{equation}\]

These will be, in practice, the main constraints, and often the only ones, that we will have to respect to construct the collision matrices. One may consider energy conservation by adding an additional conservation law [21], but we will not treat this case here.

The procedure for constructing the collision matrix indicated in  [39] or  [30] is today abandoned in favor of a method where the collision matrix is built in a basis in which it is diagonal. We will detail this method for the construction of a two-dimensional model in the following chapter.

2.3 The lattices used

The Boltzmann method is built, as we have just seen, on a regular lattice. Starting from lattice gases, two types of lattices have been defined, the hexagonal lattice in two dimensions and the face-centered hyper-cubic lattice (FCHC) in four dimensions. These models are called single-speed models because their velocity modulus \(|c_i|\) is constant. As an extension of the previous models, more complex multi-speed models have therefore been introduced. One may naturally wonder whether the projections of the FCHC model are single-speed or multi-speed models. Indeed, the FCHC model projected in two dimensions possesses nine velocities, see Fig (1.7), one with zero modulus, four with unit modulus, and finally four with modulus equal to the square root of two. Yet the FCHC model possesses twenty-four velocities of single modulus equal to the square root of two. In this section we will set up a formalism that will allow us to avoid these delicate questions. There are two types of two-dimensional lattices, triangular-based lattices and square-based lattices. Throughout the remainder of this study, we will consider only square-based lattices.
