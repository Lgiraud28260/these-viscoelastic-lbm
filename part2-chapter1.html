Part II · Viscoelasticity

Characterization of a Viscoelastic Fluid

1.1 Introduction

Viscoelastic fluids can exhibit very varied behaviors. While for an incompressible Newtonian fluid it is generally sufficient to use two constants, the dynamic viscosity \(\mu\) and the density \(\rho\), to characterize it, for a viscoelastic fluid a large number of parameters is required to describe it. Furthermore, in order to measure experimentally the value of a constant characterizing the fluid, it is useful to find an experiment that highlights it, which can easily be done for a Newtonian fluid but can become very complex for a viscoelastic fluid. For example, many states exist, steady or unsteady, that allow the dynamic viscosity of a Newtonian fluid to be measured. The major difficulty with a viscoelastic fluid is that its characteristic equations are not known in advance. If one tries, for example, to measure the viscosity of such a fluid with a viscometer, it will necessarily appear constant, whereas some fluids exhibit strong dependencies between viscosity and shear. We will therefore define a set of experiments to be performed in order to know what we shall call the characteristic parameters of the viscoelastic fluid under consideration. A summary of the main characteristics and definitions of viscoelastic fluids can be found in appendix A.

1.2 Characteristic parameters

To simplify the characterization of a viscoelastic fluid, we will choose five characteristic experiments that will allow us to define eight distinct parameters that we will call characteristic parameters of viscoelastic behavior. These characteristic parameters allow most of the important behaviors of the fluid to be predicted.

1.2.1 Couette flow

Couette flow, figure 1.1, is a classical flow of fluid mechanics. It is a flow between two parallel flat plates, one (resp. both) of which is (are) set in motion at a given velocity. Its linear and unconditionally stable character makes it a simple experiment to carry out both experimentally and numerically. In the case we have chosen, the plate at height \(y=H\) moves with a uniform velocity \(V_0\), and the other, at \(y=0\), remains fixed.

1.2.2 Non-Newtonian viscosity - Viscometric parameters

In the case of Couette flow for the incompressible case, for a Newtonian fluid, only the \(\sigma_{xy}\) component is non-zero. However, in the absence of constitutive equations for our fluid, no such assumption can be made. The most general form of the stress tensor is given by the fact that it is symmetric, which is the general case in all the studies we present here, that it is traceless and that it is invariant under the \(z\to-z\) symmetry. These conditions give us: \[\begin{equation} \sigma=\left( \begin{array}{ccc} \sigma_{xx}&\sigma_{xy}&0\\ \sigma_{xy}&\sigma_{yy}&0\\ 0&0&\sigma_{zz} \end{array} \right),\qquad\mbox{with } \sigma_{xx}+\sigma_{yy}+\sigma_{zz}=0. \end{equation}\] Three coefficients are thus required to characterize this flow. The strain rate tensor \(\dot D\) has, for symmetry reasons, only its transverse or shear component, \({\dot D}_{xy}\), non-zero, and we have: \[\begin{equation} {\dot D}_{xy}={\partial v_x(y)\over \partial y}={V_0 \over H}. \end{equation}\] It is therefore noted that the shear is constant at fixed temperature and velocity \(V_0\). This experiment gives us the non-Newtonian viscosity \(\mu({\dot \gamma})\), or shear-dependent viscosity. Indeed, by analogy with Newtonian fluids: \[\begin{equation} \sigma_{xy}=\mu({\dot \gamma})\,{\dot D}_{xy} \end{equation}\] with the shear rate \({\dot \gamma}\) defined by equation (A.23). Note that in the case of the Couette flow mentioned here, \({\dot \gamma}=|{\dot D}_{xy}|\).

In the same way, the first and second normal stress coefficients, or viscometric parameters, denoted \(\Psi_1\) and \(\Psi_2\), are defined by: \[\begin{eqnarray} \sigma_{xx}-\sigma_{yy}&=&-\Psi_1({\dot \gamma})\,{\dot D}_{xy}^2\\ \sigma_{yy}-\sigma_{zz}&=&-\Psi_2({\dot \gamma})\,{\dot D}_{xy}^2 \end{eqnarray}\]

Note that \(\mu\), \(\Psi_1\) and \(\Psi_2\) are even functions of \({\dot D}_{xy}\), which is why the power law (A.24) and the Carreau-Yasuda law (A.25) are functions of \({\dot \gamma}\).

1.2.3 Oscillatory Couette flow

Consider again the experimental setup of Couette flow, but now assuming that the velocity \(V\) of the upper plate depends on time as \[\begin{equation} V= V_0 \cos(\omega\,t). \end{equation}\]

For a Newtonian fluid, the stress is in phase with the strain rate. For a viscoelastic fluid, it is easily seen, using complex notation, that there will be a phase shift between stress and strain rate through the existence of a complex viscosity. The off-diagonal part of the stress tensor, \(\sigma_{xy}\), then takes the form: \[\begin{equation} \sigma_{xy}= A(\omega)\,\cos(\omega\,t+\phi) \end{equation}\] with \(A(\omega)=-\rho\,\sqrt{\nu'^2+\nu''^2}\) and \(\tan\phi=-{\nu''/ \nu'}\) if the kinematic viscosity has been written in complex form: \[\begin{equation} \nu^*=\nu'-\,i\,\nu''. \end{equation}\] This therefore allows us to define two additional quantities, which are the real part and the imaginary part of the viscosity.

The equation governing the motion of the fluid in a Couette channel is given by:

\[\begin{equation} \label{ac1} \rho {\partial V_x \over \partial t}=-{\partial\sigma_{xy}\over \partial y} \end{equation}\] with \[\begin{equation} \label{ac2} \sigma_{xy}=-\mu\,{\dot D}_{xy}=-\mu\,{\partial V_x \over \partial y}. \end{equation}\] Given that the flow is directed along the \(Ox\) axis, and in the absence of a pressure gradient and a driving force, for a linear-type fluid we have: \[\begin{equation} \sigma_{xx}=0. \end{equation}\] Setting \(\nu=\mu/\rho\), the kinematic shear viscosity, we finally obtain by combining equations (1.9) and (1.10): \[\begin{equation} \rho {\partial V_x \over \partial t}=\nu\, {\partial^2 V_x \over \partial y^2}. \end{equation}\] Since the motion of the upper plate is periodic, it is advantageous to use complex notation, so we can write: \[\begin{equation} {\partial^2 V_x \over \partial y^2}-k^2\,V_x=0, \end{equation}\] with \[\begin{equation} k^2={i\,\omega \over \nu^*}\qquad\mbox{where }\nu^*=\nu '-i\,\nu ''. \end{equation}\] For a viscous fluid, we will have \(\nu '=\nu\) and \(\nu ''=0\). In the following calculation, we will consider that the fluid under study has a complex viscosity. Thus, after some manipulation, we can finally find the expression of the complex components of \(k\), and setting: \[\begin{equation} k=\alpha+i\, \beta, \end{equation}\] we find: \[\begin{equation} \left\{ \begin{array}{l} \displaystyle\alpha={1\over |\nu^\star |}\sqrt{{\omega\over 2}(|\nu^\star |-\nu'')}\\ \displaystyle\beta={\nu'\over |\nu^\star |}\sqrt{{\omega\over 2(|\nu^\star |-\nu'')}} \end{array}\right. \end{equation}\]

The solution for the velocity at any point of the fluid to this problem, that is to say the velocity at height \(h\), is given by the relation: \[\begin{equation} V_x(y)=V_0\,\mbox{Re}\left({\sinh(k\,y)\over \sinh(k\,H) }e^{i\,\omega\,t}\right). \end{equation}\] For simplicity of notation let us set: \[\begin{equation} {\sinh(k\,y)\over \sinh(k\,H) }=\alpha_1+i\,\beta_1, \end{equation}\] we can then write the solution of this problem in the simplified form: \[\begin{equation} V_x(y)=B(\omega,y)\,\cos (\omega\, t +\phi ), \end{equation}\] with \(\displaystyle B(\omega,y)=V_0\,\sqrt{\alpha_1^2+\beta_1^2}\) and \(\displaystyle\phi=\arctan{\beta_1\over \alpha_1}\) .

1.3 Oscillatory or Pulsed Poiseuille Flow

A second example which will also allow us to control the real and imaginary parts of the viscosity is that of Poiseuille flow. A Poiseuille flow is a flow between two parallel and fixed flat plates separated by a height \(H\) as indicated in Fig.(1.3). To create a pulsation effect, one can take a pressure gradient oscillating in time. In the linear case, this is equivalent to taking a volumetric forcing term oscillating as a function of time.

The case of oscillatory Poiseuille flow, or pulsed Poiseuille flow, differs only slightly from the previous study. Indeed, the Stokes equation for Poiseuille flow is similar to the one employed previously. The only difference lies in the appearance of a pressure forcing term or of a volumetric force term which we will denote \(F(t)=F \cos(\omega t)\). Thus, one has to solve \[\begin{equation} \label{ac3} \rho {\partial V_x \over \partial t}=F(t)-{\partial\sigma_{xy}\over \partial y}, \end{equation}\] where one still has \[\begin{equation} \label{ac4} \sigma_{xy}=-\mu\,{\dot D}_{xy}=-\mu\,{\partial V_x \over \partial y}. \end{equation}\] In the same way as before, in view of the symmetries of the problem, we have: \[\begin{equation} \sigma_{xx}=0. \end{equation}\]

Using complex notation, the equation we have to solve becomes: \[\begin{equation} {\partial^2 V_x \over \partial y^2}-k^2\,V_x=-{F\over \nu^\star}, \end{equation}\] the expression of \(k\) remains the same, namely: \[\begin{equation} k=\alpha+i\, \beta, \end{equation}\] with: \[\begin{equation} \left\{ \begin{array}{l} \displaystyle\alpha={1\over |\nu^\star |}\sqrt{{\omega\over 2}(|\nu^\star |-\nu'')}\\ \displaystyle\beta={\nu'\over |\nu^\star |}\sqrt{{\omega\over 2(|\nu^\star |-\nu'')}} \end{array}\right. \end{equation}\]

The solution of equations (1.20) and (1.21) in terms of velocity is given by: \[\begin{equation} V_x=\mbox{Re}\left[ e^{i\,\omega\,t}{F\over i \omega}\left({e^{k\,y}-1-e^{k\,H}+e^{k\,(H-y)} \over 1+e^{k\,H} } \right)\right]. \end{equation}\] For simplicity of notation, setting: \[\begin{equation} {e^{k\,y}-1-e^{k\,H}+e^{k\,(H-y)} \over 1+e^{k\,H} } =\alpha_1+i\,\beta_1, \end{equation}\] we then have: \[\begin{equation} V_x=A\,\sin(\omega\,t+\phi), \end{equation}\] with \(\displaystyle A(\omega,y)={F \over \omega}\,\sqrt{\alpha_1^2+\beta_1^2}\) and \(\displaystyle\phi=\arctan{\beta_1\over \alpha_1}\).

1.3.1 Transient parameters

In the following three experiments, we will define three parameters that come into play in the transient establishment of the flow, namely \(\nu^{+}\), \(\nu^{-}\) and \(\nu^G\). These experiments can be defined from the previous Couette-type flows, taking as initial condition, for the non-zero component of the strain rate tensor, a non-periodic function of the Heaviside or Dirac type. In fact, everything happens as if one were actuating one of the two plates of the Couette device and the fluid immediately felt its effects. As these motions are no longer periodic in nature, we can no longer use the complex viscosity. We could therefore use Laplace transforms to solve these three problems analytically. The solutions thus obtained are difficult to exploit because they are in integral form. We will therefore prefer to use the equation relating stress and strain rate. Indeed, it is simpler to integrate by making the assumption that the fluid immediately feels in volume the action of the plate.

1.3.1.1 Activation of shear from a fluid at rest

The first case is that in which the fluid is at rest until time \(t_0\), from which the upper plate is pulled in such a way that the shear is constant and non-zero throughout the fluid.

This experiment will allow us to define the transient parameter \(\nu^{+}\) by: \[\begin{equation} \sigma_{xy}=-\nu^{+}\,{\dot D}_{xy}. \end{equation}\] The parameter \(\nu^{+}\) will therefore depend strongly on the nature of the fluid under consideration. Indeed, for the case of a Jeffreys fluid, whose characteristics are given in appendix A, but whose constitutive equation we can recall: \[\begin{equation} \sigma+\,\tau_1 {\partial \sigma \over \partial t}=-\mu_0\,\left({\dot D}+\tau_2\,{\partial {\dot D} \over \partial t} \right), \end{equation}\] and with the shear given by: \[\begin{equation} {\dot D}_{xy}(t)={\dot D}_0\,H(t), \end{equation}\] \(H(t)\) being the Heaviside function, it is then found that the stress \(\sigma_{xy}\) is given by: \[\begin{eqnarray} \sigma_{xy}&=&-\mu_0\,{\dot D}_0\,{\tau_2\over \tau_1}-\mu_0\,{\dot D}_0\,\left(1-{\tau_2\over \tau_1} \right)\,\left( 1-e^{-t/\tau_1}\right),\\ &=&-\nu^{+}\,{\dot D}_0. \end{eqnarray}\]

1.3.1.2 Cessation of shear from an already sheared fluid

The second case is that in which the shear is suddenly stopped at time \(t=0\) after it had been established at a constant non-zero value for “infinitely” long as illustrated in figure(1.5).

This experiment will allow us to define the transient parameter \(\nu^{-}\) by: \[\begin{equation} \sigma_{xy}=-\nu^{-}\,{\dot D}_{xy}. \end{equation}\] For a Jeffreys fluid we then have: \[\begin{equation} \nu^{-}=-\mu_0\,{\dot D}_0\left(1-{\tau_2\over \tau_1} \right)\,e^{-t/\tau_1}. \end{equation}\]

1.3.1.3 Sudden shear

The last case is that in which the shear is sudden, that is to say, the time elapsed between the moment when it is established and the moment when it disappears is very short. The shape of this curve can be mathematically represented by a Dirac “delta” function, but physically this function is hardly accessible, so we will take the shape of the curve shown in figure(1.6).

This experiment will allow us to define the transient parameter \(\nu^{G}\) by: \[\begin{equation} \sigma_{xy}=-\nu^{G}\,{\dot D}_{xy}. \end{equation}\]

It can then be shown that for a time interval \(\delta t\ll 1\), we find: \[\begin{equation} \nu^{G}=\delta t\, G(t-t_0), \end{equation}\] where \(G(t-t_0)\) is the relaxation modulus of the fluid, defined for a Jeffreys fluid in appendix A: \[\begin{equation} G(t-t_0)=\mu_0\left(1-{\tau_2 \over \tau_1} \right)\,e^{-(t-t_0)/\tau_1} + 2 {\mu_0\,\tau_2 \over \tau_1}\,\delta(t-t_0). \end{equation}\]

Other transient parameters can also be defined, such as \(\Psi^+\), \(\Psi^-\) or \(\Psi^G\), to quantify the transient responses of the two normal stresses. Thus, six additional parameters are obtained. However, we will not address these various parameters in this thesis and we will limit ourselves, for the sake of concision, to the eight main parameters presented previously, \(\mu\), \(\nu'\), \(\nu''\), \(\Psi_1\), \(\Psi_2\), \(\nu^+\), \(\nu^-\) and finally \(\nu^G\).
