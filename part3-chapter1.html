Part III · Modeling of Viscoelasticity with the Lattice Boltzmann Method — Chapter 1

Basic principles

Let us recall, first of all, that a Newtonian viscous fluid responds, by a nonzero stress, to a variation of the deformation, and, calling \(\sigma_{ij}\) the stress and \({\dot D}_{ij}=\partial_i v_j+\partial_j v_i\) the deformation rate as a function of the local velocity \(v\), we then have as the constitutive law: \[\begin{equation} \label{Newton-comp} \sigma_{ij}=- \mu \, {\dot D}_{ij}, \end{equation}\] the constant \(\mu\) denoting the dynamic shear viscosity.

The first idea one can have of a viscoelastic fluid is that it keeps, for a sufficiently long time, the memory of the stresses it has undergone in its past. The simplest equation to describe this phenomenon is the equation of Maxwell  [8], where one makes the assumption that the memory of the fluid decays exponentially: \[\begin{equation} \label{Maxwell-comp} \sigma_{ij}+\tau_1\,{\partial \sigma_{ij} \over \partial t }=-\mu \, {\dot D}_{ij}, \end{equation}\] the quantity \(\tau_1\) being then a relaxation time. This equation integrates to: \[\begin{equation} \label{Maxwell-comp-inter} \sigma_{ij}=-\int_{-\infty}^t{\left\{ {\mu\over \tau_1} e^{-{t-t'\over \tau_1}}\right\} {\dot D_{ij}} dt' }. \end{equation}\] One can already note that if the fluid loses memory very rapidly, \(\tau_1\) becomes small, this being the assumption made for classical fluids, and then equation (1.2) does tend toward the equation describing the behaviour of Newtonian fluids.

From the set of \(n\) populations at each lattice point, one can build \(n\) modes or moments by linear combinations. The lattice Boltzmann scheme will then lead these modes to undergo a relaxation toward an equilibrium state. The relaxation times of these modes determine the different characters of the fluid, such as viscosity. They are introduced as the eigenvalues of the collision operator. When one performs a Chapman-Enskog expansion of the lattice Boltzmann models, one can isolate several moments linked to the components of the deformation rate tensor \({\dot D}\). Take the example of the two-dimensional model. Equations (3.18) and (3.19), for the moments denoted \(S_{xx}\) and \(S_{xy}\) in the expansion we carried out in part 3, can easily be rewritten: \[\begin{eqnarray} S_{xx}^{(1)}+{1\over\lambda_\nu}\,{\partial S_{xx}^{(0)}\over \partial t_1}&=&-{\rho\over 3 \lambda_\nu} {\dot D}_{xx}^*,\\ S_{xy}^{(1)}+{1\over\lambda_\nu}\,{\partial S_{xy}^{(0)}\over \partial t_1}&=&-{\rho\over 3 \lambda_\nu} {\dot D}_{xy}^*. \end{eqnarray}\] The tensor \({\mathbf {\dot D}^*}\) is related to the deformation rate tensor and to the space dimension, \(d\), by: \[\begin{equation} \rho\,{ \dot{\mathbf{D}}}^*=\rho\,{\dot{\mathbf{D}}}-\frac{2}{d}\,{\mathbf{I}}\, \left(\nabla\cdot {\mathbf{j}} \right). \end{equation}\] The tensors \({\mathbf {\dot D}^*}\) and \({\mathbf {\dot D}}\) coincide in the case of an incompressible fluid.

Up to now, in the study of the viscous cases, we had taken \(\lambda_\nu\) of order 1, which corresponds to a short relaxation time. The quantities \(S_{\alpha\beta}^{(0)}\) were then of order \(u^2\) or zero, which has the effect of eliminating the time-derivative terms of these quantities and of recovering the classical Navier-Stokes equations. Indeed, if one makes this type of hypothesis, we can deduce the constitutive equation of our lattice Boltzmann gas: indeed, we then have: \[\begin{eqnarray} S_{xx}^{(1)}&=&-{\rho \over 3 \lambda_\nu} {\dot D}_{xx}^*,\\ S_{xy}^{(1)}&=&-{\rho \over 3 \lambda_\nu} {\dot D}_{xy}^*, \end{eqnarray}\] and, taking the kinematic shear viscosity \(\nu\) as: \[\begin{equation} \nu={1\over 3}\left({1\over \lambda_\nu}-{1\over 2} \right)={\mu\over \rho}, \end{equation}\] and by identifying the components of the stress tensor \(\sigma\), this leads us to: \[\begin{eqnarray} \sigma_{xx}&=&\left(1-{\lambda_\nu\over 2}\right)\, S_{xx}^{(1)}=-\mu \, {\dot D}_{xx}^*,\\ \sigma_{xy}&=&\left(1-{\lambda_\nu\over 2}\right)\, S_{xy}^{(1)}=-\mu \, {\dot D}_{xx}^*. \end{eqnarray}\] This is indeed the constitutive equation of a Newtonian fluid when one is placed in the incompressible limit.

We will adapt to the lattice Boltzmann gas the idea already presented in  [18]. Thus, after the addition of extra internal variables to the viscous modes, we will let them relax with a very long, even infinite, relaxation time. The relaxation times of the viscous modes, which are traditionally short, will then become long, and this will guarantee a memory effect in the fluid. We will thus create temporal memory effects.
