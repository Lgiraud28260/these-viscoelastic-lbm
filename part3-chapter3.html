Part III · Modeling of Viscoelasticity with the Lattice Boltzmann Method — Chapter 3

Isotropic fifteen-velocity model

This model is based on a thirteen-velocity model. To familiarize ourselves with this lattice, we will first build a viscous (Newtonian) model, then we will add internal variables to model the viscoelastic aspects.

3.1 Presentation of the model

The thirteen-velocity model based on the lattice defined in figure 3.1 can be identified by the list of eigenvectors of the collision matrix as well as by its eigenvalues: \[\begin{eqnarray} \lambda_\sigma, \lambda_\sigma, \lambda_\chi,\lambda_\chi,\lambda_\chi',\lambda_\chi',\lambda_z \right\}. \end{equation}\] One can note that the vectors \({\mathbf{e}}_{4}\) and \({\mathbf{e}}_{5}\) are proportional to the standard vectors \(Q_{xx}\) and \(Q_{xy}\), where \(Q_{i\,\alpha\beta}=c_{i\,\alpha}c_{i\,\beta}-c_i^2/2\, \delta_{\alpha\beta}\) is the symmetric traceless tensor linked to the viscous stress tensor, which is why the modes \(S_{xx}\) and \(S_{xy}\) associated with them will be linked to the components of this tensor.

One can also note that this type of lattice allows one to obtain associated tensors that are isotropic up to order six.

We can now write the equations coming from the Chapman-Enskog expansion for the first and second orders. One then notices that it is advantageous to set, for the variables expressed in the new basis: \[\begin{eqnarray} \label{defmom} \phantom{+}\widehat{N}_{0}=\rho/\sqrt{13}\,,\quad&\phantom{+}\widehat{N}_{1}=j_x/\sqrt{14}\,,&\quad\phantom{+}\widehat{N}_{2}= j_y/\sqrt{14}\nonumber\\ \phantom{+}\widehat{N}_{3}=\sqrt{{13 \over 77}}\left({14 \over 13}\rho-e\right)\,,\quad&\phantom{+}\widehat{N}_{4}=S_{xx}/2 \sqrt{5},&\quad\phantom{+}\widehat{N}_{5}= S_{xy}/2\nonumber\\ \phantom{+}\widehat{N}_{6}=S_1/6\,,\quad&\phantom{+}\widehat{N}_{7}=S_2/6\sqrt{77}\,,&\quad\phantom{+}\widehat{N}_{8}= q_{x1}/2\sqrt{3}\nonumber\\ \phantom{+}\widehat{N}_{9}=q_{y1}/2\sqrt{3}\,,\quad&\phantom{+}\widehat{N}_{10}=q_{x2}/\sqrt{42}\,, &\quad\phantom{+}\widehat{N}_{11}=q_{y2}/\sqrt{42}\nonumber\\ \phantom{+}\widehat{N}_{12}=T/2\sqrt{5}.\quad\nonumber& \end{eqnarray}\]

To recover the isotropic Navier-Stokes equations, the equilibrium values of the moments \(q^{(0)}_\alpha\) can be chosen as: \[\begin{eqnarray} &q^{(0)}_{\alpha\,i}=\kappa_i j_\alpha,\quad \left\{\begin{array}{l} \alpha \in \{x,y\}\\ i \in\{1,2\} \end{array}\right.\displaystyle\mbox{with }\kappa_1={15 \kappa -16 \over 6}\displaystyle \mbox{ and } \kappa_2={21 \kappa -38 \over 12} \end{eqnarray}\] where \(\kappa\) is a free parameter of the model. If one chooses for the equation of state of the fluid \(P=c_s^2\,\rho\), where \(P\) is the pressure, and if one wants a Galilean-invariant model, then one must have: \[\begin{eqnarray} &&S^{(0)}_{xx}=\frac{1}{\rho}\left( j_x^2-j_y^2 \right),\\ &&S^{(0)}_{xy}=\frac{1}{\rho} j_xj_y,\\ &&e^{(0)}=c_s^2 \rho+\frac{1}{2 \rho}\left( j_x^2+j_y^2 \right),\\ &&T^{(0)}=\frac{\theta}{ \rho}\left( j_x^2-j_y^2 \right), \end{eqnarray}\] where \(c_s\) and \(\theta\) are also free parameters of the model while having \(c_s^2<\kappa\). For simplicity reasons we will choose \[\begin{eqnarray} &&S_1^{(0)}=\left(4+c_s^2\left( {19 \kappa \over 2}-{46 \over 3} \right) \right)\rho,\\ &&S_2^{(0)}=\left(28+c_s^2\left( {77 \kappa \over 4}-{365 \over 6} \right) \right)\rho. \end{eqnarray}\] and \(\lambda_\nu=\lambda'_\nu\), which gives for shear and bulk viscosity: \[\nu=\frac{\kappa}{2}\left({1 \over \lambda_\nu}-{1 \over 2}\right), \mbox{\quad and \quad} \xi=\left(\kappa-c_s^2 \right) \left({1 \over \lambda_e}-{1 \over 2} \right).\] One can note that this model projects onto the nine-velocity model (D2Q9) when one chooses: \(\kappa=2/3\), \(c_s^2=1/3\), \(\theta=-4\), and if, in addition, the populations \(N_9\) to \(N_{12}\) are taken to be zero at \(t=0\).

3.2 The 15-velocity model

We have already seen that it was necessary to add two internal variables to the nine-velocity model to ensure an infinite viscosity for it, which leads us to consider now a fifteen-velocity model. We will therefore build the four viscous modes from the following eigenvectors: \[\begin{eqnarray} {\tilde{\mathbf{Q}}_{xx}}&=&\left(\;\;\phantom{1}\phantom{-} \phantom{1}a,\phantom{1}\phantom{-} \phantom{1}0,\phantom{-}0,\phantom{-}1,-1,\phantom{-}1,-1, \phantom{-}0,\phantom{-}0,\phantom{-}0,\phantom{-}0,\phantom{-}4,-4,\phantom{-}4,-4\right),\nonumber\\ {\tilde{\mathbf{Q}}_{xy}}&=&\left(\;\;\phantom{1}\phantom{-} \phantom{1}0,\phantom{1}\phantom{-} \phantom{1}b,\phantom{-}0,\phantom{-}0,\phantom{-}0,\phantom{-}0,\phantom{-}0, \phantom{-}1,-1,\phantom{-}1,-1,\phantom{-}0,\phantom{-}0,\phantom{-}0,\phantom{-}0\right),\nonumber\\ {\tilde{\mathbf{Q}}_{xx}}^\bot&=&\left({-68/ a},\phantom{1}\phantom{-} \phantom{1}0,\phantom{-}0,\phantom{-}1,-1,\phantom{-}1,-1, \phantom{-}0,\phantom{-}0,\phantom{-}0,\phantom{-}0,\phantom{-}4,-4,\phantom{-}4,-4\right),\nonumber\\ {\tilde{\mathbf{Q}}_{xy}}^\bot&=&\left(\;\;\phantom{1}\phantom{-} \phantom{1}0,-4/ b,\phantom{-}0,\phantom{-}0,\phantom{-}0,\phantom{-}0,\phantom{-}0, \phantom{-}1,-1,\phantom{-}1,-1,\phantom{-}0,\phantom{-}0,\phantom{-}0,\phantom{-}0\right),\nonumber \end{eqnarray}\] which we have not normalized for readability reasons and where \(a\) and \(b\) are again parameters of the model. These eigenvectors will be associated with the eigenvalues: \[\{\lambda_\nu,\lambda'_\nu,\lambda_\gamma,\lambda_\gamma'\}.\]

3.3 The quasi-elastic 15-velocity model

If one places oneself in the case where the modes \(P_{\alpha\beta}\) are strictly conserved, thus \(\lambda_\gamma=\lambda_\gamma'=0\), one obtains a quasi-elastic model. After manipulation of the equations resulting from the Chapman-Enskog expansion, one obtains the following dynamic equations: \[\begin{eqnarray} \label{cont} &&\partial_t\rho + \partial_xj_x+\partial_yj_y=0,\\ \label{NSx} &&\partial_tj_x+\partial_xP+ {c_\perp^2 \over \kappa}\,(\partial_xP_{xx}+\partial_yP_{xy})\nonumber\\ &&\phantom{\partial_tj_x} =\nu_\infty\,\Delta j_x+\xi_\infty\,\partial_x(\partial_xj_x+\partial_yj_y),\qquad\qquad\\ \label{NSy} &&\partial_tj_y+\partial_yP+ {c_\perp^2 \over \kappa}\,(\partial_xP_{xy}-\partial_yP_{xx}) \nonumber\\ &&\phantom{\partial_tj_x} =\nu_\infty\,\Delta j_y+\xi_\infty\,\partial_y(\partial_xj_x+\partial_yj_y),\\ &&\partial_tP_{xx}+ \kappa\,(\partial_xj_x-\partial_yj_y) = D_\infty \,\Delta P_{xx},\\ \label{viscom} &&\partial_tP_{xy}+ \kappa\,(\partial_xj_y+\partial_yj_x) = D_\infty \,\Delta P_{xy}. \end{eqnarray}\]

One notices that this time, the coefficient in front of the Laplacian is identical for the two modes \(P\). Moreover, we must choose, for the same isotropy reasons: \[\begin{eqnarray} &&a^2={136 \,c_\perp^2 \over \kappa-2c_\perp^2},\qquad\qquad b^2={4 \,c_\perp^2 \over \kappa-c_\perp^2},\\ &&\lambda'_\chi= {(749-442\,\kappa)\lambda_\chi \over (317-442\,\kappa)+216\lambda_\chi},\\ \mbox{and}&&\lambda_\nu^{'}={4 (\kappa-c_\perp^2)\lambda_\nu \over 2(\kappa-2c_\perp^2)+\kappa\,\lambda_\nu}, \end{eqnarray}\] where \(\kappa\) and \(c_\perp\) are free parameters of the model, which must satisfy the relations \(0<\kappa<749/442\) and \(c_{\perp}^2<\kappa/2<749/884\). One can note that we have had to take \(\lambda_\nu\neq\lambda'_\nu\). For isotropy reasons and simplicity of writing, we choose for the equilibrium values: \[\begin{eqnarray} &q^{(0)}_{\alpha\,i}=\kappa_i j_\alpha,\quad \left\{\begin{array}{l} \alpha \in \{x,y\}\\ i \in\{1,2\} \end{array}\right.\mbox{ with }\displaystyle\kappa_1={13 \kappa -8 \over 3}\quad\displaystyle \mbox{and }\kappa_2={14 \kappa -19 \over 6},\qquad \\ &\displaystyle S_1^{(0)}=\left(4+(47\kappa-46){c_s^2 \over 3} \right)\rho,\\ &\displaystyle S_2^{(0)}=\left(28+(154\kappa-365){c_s^2 \over 6}\right)\rho. \end{eqnarray}\] We note that this choice is the one that allows us to fall directly back onto equations similar to those of the eleven-velocity model, but there exist other values of \(\kappa_1\) and \(\kappa_2\) allowing one to obtain an isotropic model. To obtain the desired equation of state of the gas, \(P=c_s^2\,\rho\), we must give the equilibrium value of \(e\): \[\begin{equation} e^{(0)}=c_s^2\,\rho \end{equation}\] where \(c_s\) remains a free parameter of the model with the restriction \(c_s^2<3\kappa/2\). The shear and bulk viscosities, the coefficient \(D_\infty\), as well as the pressure are given by: \[\begin{eqnarray} &&\nu_\infty= ({\kappa\over 2}-c_{\perp}^2)\,({1\over \lambda_\nu}-{1\over 2}),\qquad\qquad \xi_\infty=({3\kappa\over 2}-c_s^2)\,({1\over \lambda_e}-{1\over 2}),\\ &&D_\infty=c_{\perp}^2\,{845-1383\,\kappa+442\,\kappa^2 \over \kappa(749-442\,\kappa)}\,({1\over \lambda_\chi}-{1\over 2}), \qquad\mbox{and \qquad}P=c_s^2\,\rho.\qquad\qquad \end{eqnarray}\]

Like the eleven-velocity model, the fifteen-velocity model is not Galilean invariant. This problem being very strongly linked to that of the introduction of nonlinear terms and of convective derivatives, we will treat this problem later.

3.4 The viscous 15-velocity model

The transition from the elastic model to the viscous case is done by assuming that the eigenvalues \(\lambda_\gamma\) and \(\lambda_\gamma'\) are of order one, and one can take without loss of generality \(\lambda_\gamma=\lambda_\gamma'\). For continuity reasons, we will keep the same equilibrium quantities for the viscous case as for the elastic case. We must specify the equilibrium values of the two \(P\) modes. We will take the simplest choice at our disposal, that is: \[\begin{eqnarray} &&P^{(0)}_{xx}=P^{(0)}_{xy}=0. \end{eqnarray}\] One then recovers the Stokes equations with, now for the shear viscosity, \[\begin{equation} \label{viscosite-0} \nu_0=({\kappa\over 2}-c_{\perp}^2)\,({1\over \lambda_\nu}-{1\over 2})+ c_{\perp}^2\,({1\over \lambda_\gamma}-{1 \over 2}). \end{equation}\]

3.5 The viscoelastic 15-velocity model

If one takes back the equilibrium quantities as well as the isotropy conditions of the two previous cases, one can make the hypothesis that when the eigenvalues \(\lambda_\gamma\) and \(\lambda_\gamma'\) are small but not strictly zero, the isotropy conditions still remain valid. We can then check, through the dispersion-equation method, as for the eleven-velocity model, that our model is isotropic up to relatively large values of the wavenumber \(k\), as indicated in figure 3.2. In the same way as for the eleven-velocity model, the transition from the viscous behaviour to a viscoelastic behaviour occurs around \(\displaystyle k_c=\frac{\lambda_\gamma}{2\,c_{\perp}}\).

This model will be used for all the simulations in the rest of this manuscript.
